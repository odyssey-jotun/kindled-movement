## My story

### I used to count myself out

I spent years avoiding exercise on purpose. I was so fixed on how I looked next
to everyone else that I never gave it a fair shot, and every workout felt like
proof I didn't measure up.

What changed was not finding the right workout. It was finding a reason to move
that I could actually live with. I started paying attention to how movement made
me feel instead of how it made me look, and it became something I wanted rather
than something I owed.

> I know what it's like to decide you are not the kind of person who does this.

That's the whole reason this class exists.

---

## Why so many of us stop

Most women I talk to have tried. They signed up, went for a while, and it turned
into something they dreaded. A wall of mirrors. A room that felt like a
comparison. A scoreboard that measured the wrong thing.

Somewhere in there, moving stops being about what your body can do and starts
being about whether you are good enough. When it doesn't deliver the thing you
were told to want, quitting is the reasonable response.

> So you count yourself out.

None of the good part depends on what you weigh. That's the part that gets lost.

## What the research shows

I went and read the studies before I built this. Here is what they actually found.

**Structured movement lifted self-esteem by 41.8% over six weeks**, nearly double
the gain from a support group alone, in a randomized trial of women who had
experienced domestic violence. Exercise was the only arm with significant gains
in physical condition. (Legrand & Crombez-Bequet, 2022)

**Breath-focused, body-aware movement significantly lowered stress-symptom
scores** against an active control, in a randomized trial of women with PTSD.
Depression and anxiety scores were lower too. (Yi et al., 2022)

**Those gains held at a three-month follow-up.** (Yi et al., 2022)

**Peer connection is part of the mechanism, not a bonus.** The STEM running
program treats moving alongside other people as an active ingredient in the
result. (Maniccia & Leone, 2019)

**Smaller doses work.** In a trial of home-based movement, 60 minutes a week was
feasible and well accepted, while the heavier commitment was not. An hour a week
is a real dose. (Huberty et al., 2020)

### One honest note

Those numbers come from programs that ran six to twelve weeks, not from a single
hour. One class is a first exposure and a demonstration of how the method works.
The research is the case for keeping it up, and keeping it up is the point.

## How the hour works

**Settle in — 10 minutes.** Breath and body awareness, then guided mobility.
Hips, spine, shoulders. Nothing strenuous, nothing to keep up with.

**Move at your own pace — 25 minutes.** Walking or light cardio, in rounds, at an
effort where you can still talk but not sing. Every round has a seated version.

**Build strength — 15 minutes.** Simple bodyweight work, modified for every
ability. Squats or sit-to-stands, pushups at the wall or the floor, planks on
forearms or knees.

**Release — 10 minutes.** Longer holds and a closing practice to let the tension
go before you walk back out.

No experience necessary. Equipment is provided. A mat is all this needs.

## What the room is like

This part matters as much as the plan.

- Every movement has a seated, kneeling, or lying version, and stopping is always allowed.
- No mirrors, no hands-on corrections, and the exits stay visible.
- No talk about weight or appearance. Not from me, and not in the room.
- A gentle stretch-and-tone track runs the whole hour if you would rather not raise your heart rate in a group.
- You work next to a partner, because the research says the company is part of why it works.
- Childcare wherever the host can provide it.
