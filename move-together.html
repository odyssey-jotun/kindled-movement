I used to count myself out

I spent years avoiding exercise on purpose. I was so fixed on how I looked next to everyone else that I never gave it a fair shot, and every workout felt like proof I didn't measure up.

What changed was not finding the right workout. It was finding a reason to move that I could actually live with. I started paying attention to how movement made me feel instead of how it made me look, and it became something I wanted rather than something I owed.

I know what it's like to decide you are not the kind of person who does this.

That's the whole reason this class exists.

Why so many of us stop

Most women I talk to have tried. They signed up, went for a while, and it turned into something they dreaded. A wall of mirrors. A room that felt like a comparison. A scoreboard that measured the wrong thing.

Somewhere in there, moving stops being about what your body can do and starts being about whether you are good enough. When it doesn't deliver the thing you were told to want, quitting is the reasonable response.

So you count yourself out.

I don't think anybody should have to feel that way about their own body. None of the good part depends on what you weigh, and that's the part that gets lost.

What the research actually says

I went and read the studies before I built this. Randomized trials, the kind with control groups. Here is what they found.

  1. 01

    Self-esteem rose 41.8% in six weeks

    In a randomized trial, structured movement lifted global self-esteem nearly double what a support group alone did, and it was the only arm with significant gains in physical condition.

  2. 02

    Breath-focused movement lowers stress

    Against an active control, body-aware movement produced significantly lower stress-symptom scores, along with lower depression and anxiety. Those gains still held at a three-month follow-up.

  3. 03

    An hour a week is a real dose

    In a trial of home-based movement, 60 minutes a week was feasible and well accepted while the heavier commitment was not. Moving alongside other people is part of the mechanism, not a bonus.

One honest note

Those numbers come from programs that ran six to twelve weeks, not from a single hour. One class is a first exposure and a demonstration of how the method works. The research is the case for keeping it up, and keeping it up is the point.

How the hour works

No experience necessary. Equipment is provided, and a mat is all this needs.

  1. 1

    Settle in

    Ten minutes of breath and body awareness, then guided mobility. Hips, spine, shoulders. Nothing strenuous and nothing to keep up with.

  2. 2

    Move at your own pace

    Twenty-five minutes of walking or light cardio in rounds, at an effort where you can still talk but not sing. Every round has a seated version.

  3. 3

    Build strength

    Fifteen minutes of simple bodyweight work, modified for every ability. Squats or sit-to-stands, pushups at the wall or the floor, planks on forearms or knees.

  4. 4

    Release

    Ten minutes of longer holds and a closing practice, to let the tension go before you walk back out.

Every movement has a seated, kneeling, or lying version, and stopping is always allowed. No mirrors, no hands-on corrections, and no talk about weight or appearance. Not from me, and not in the room. That's my promise.

Waiting costs you

You can keep putting it off. That's the easy road, and there's no shame in having taken it this long. I took it for years.

The part that stings is what you miss in the meantime. The steadier mood, the strength that builds so slowly you don't notice until one day you do, and the confidence that comes from being on good terms with your own body. None of it shows up on its own.

The five studies this is built on

I read these in full and wrote up what each one actually found, what it could not tell me, and what it changed about the hour. Here they are in short.

  1. Legrand, F. D., & Crombez-Bequet, N. (2022). Physical exercise and self-esteem in women facing lockdown-related domestic violence. Journal of Family Violence, 37(6), 1005–1011.

    A randomized trial of 36 women, all inactive at the start, split between exercise, a counseling support group, and no intervention. Six weeks. Global self-esteem rose 41.8% in the exercise group against 21.7% in the support group, and the exercise group was the only one to gain significantly in physical condition.

    In the hour The 25 minutes of cardio and 10 of bodyweight strength come straight from this protocol, as does letting you choose your own type and pace.

  2. Maniccia, D. M., & Leone, J. M. (2019). Theoretical framework and protocol for the evaluation of Strong Through Every Mile. BMC Public Health, 19(1), 693.

    The design for a 10-week community running program for survivors of intimate partner violence, with a published model linking group movement to social support and self-efficacy. This is a protocol, so it reports no outcomes yet.

    In the hour You work next to a partner. The company is treated as part of the method, not as decoration.

  3. Yi, L., Lian, Y., Ma, N., & Duan, N. (2022). A randomized controlled trial of the influence of yoga for women with symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder. Journal of Translational Medicine, 20(1), 162.

    94 women with diagnosed PTSD, randomized to 12 weeks of Kripalu-based yoga or an active control. Trauma-symptom scores were significantly lower in the yoga group, along with depression and anxiety, and those gains held at a three-month follow-up.

    In the hour The shape of the opening and closing blocks: breath first, then noticing sensation, then attending to what the body feels rather than how it looks, with holds kept long.

  4. Huberty, J., Sullivan, M., Green, J., Kurka, J., Leiferman, J., Gold, K., & Cacciatore, J. (2020). Online yoga to reduce post traumatic stress in women who have experienced stillbirth. BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies, 20(1), 173.

    90 women, randomized across 60 minutes a week, 150 minutes a week, and a gentle stretch-and-tone control. The lighter dose was feasible and well accepted. The heavier one was reported as too much.

    In the hour One hour a week is the target, and a gentle stretch-and-tone track runs the whole session for anyone who wants it.

  5. Calle-Guisado, V., et al. (2023). Effects of a multicomponent programme for improving physical and psychological health in victims of intimate partner violence. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 20(4), 2815.

    The design for a four-week program pairing outdoor activity with group therapy, measuring self-esteem and body image alongside salivary cortisol. Also a protocol, with no results reported yet.

    In the hour Childcare wherever the host can manage it, which this team wrote in specifically to stop people dropping out.

What these studies cannot tell you

Two of the five are protocols, so they describe a design rather than a result, and I cite them for the framework and not as evidence of effect. One studied bereaved mothers rather than survivors of violence, so I lean on it for how to deliver a program, not for who it helps. And every measured result here came from six to twelve weeks of sessions, not from one.
